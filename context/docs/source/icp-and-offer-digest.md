# Rave GTM — ICP & Offer Digest

Source: "Outbound Strategy Brief — Local Website Revamp Agency", Rave GTM, July 2026.
Companion to [rave-launch-plan.md](rave-launch-plan.md), which covers infrastructure.

---

## The business in one line

Find local service businesses whose website is quietly costing them jobs, email the
owner directly, offer to *show* them a finished redesign for free with no
commitment. If they like it, build the real thing in a few days and get paid.

**The job of the outreach:** get an owner curious enough to take a 15-minute call.
Then the finished design and the math do the selling.

---

## Why this is a strong bet

1. Showing something specific and finished beats a generic pitch — the owner can
   judge it in seconds without trusting a word we say.
2. Small owner-run businesses reply ~3x as often as employees at larger companies.
   Nearly every target is owner-run. This is the easiest audience in cold outreach
   to get a response from.

---

## The offer

**What they actually buy:** proof, a finished asset, and zero risk — wrapped around
more booked jobs from traffic they already get.

### Credibility stack
- Named case studies with hard numbers. a moving company: 21% more inbound calls, 17
  booked moves in 30 days. Restoration: 22% more booked jobs, no added ad spend.
  Real domains, real results.
- Volume proof: "I've helped 150+ local service providers book more jobs from
  their site."
- A finished design they can actually see, built for their business type before
  we ever reach out.

### Emotional drivers
| Driver | How it is used |
|---|---|
| Zero risk | "If you like it and want it up by next week, I'll set it up and pay for it myself." |
| Fear of lost jobs | "Your site isn't broken — but it's losing you jobs you should be winning." |
| Pride / recognition | "You're clearly great at what you do, but your website is downplaying that." Flatter the work, indict the site, never the owner. |
| Speed | "Up by next week." Not a months-long agency project. |
| Free leads sweetener | Email 3: "...and I'll send you 5 [local customers] you can close into jobs." |

### Pricing — DECIDED 2026-08-11

**$2,000 one-time build + $97/mo care plan.** One price, no tiers.

Pitch it as included by default, not an upsell:
*"$2,000 for the build, then $97/month and I host it, maintain it, and make any
changes you need — cancel whenever."*

Reasoning:
- Targets have job values of $2k-$30k+. An owner whose average job is $8,000 does
  not evaluate $1,500 and $2,000 differently. $1,500 gives up 25% of revenue for no
  conversion gain.
- The anchor being beaten is $5-10k over months. $2,000 delivered in a week is
  still a 75-80% discount.
- Cheap is a scam signal. The #2 objection is "this sounds like a scam" — a stranger
  offering a full custom site for $1,500 reads *more* suspicious, not less.
- $3,000 is defensible later, once real case studies and testimonials exist. With
  zero closed deals and borrowed proof, $2,000 is the honest ceiling.

**No packages yet.** Three tiers fight the pitch ("one short call, everything
handled"). They turn a 15-minute call into a comparison exercise and give a busy
owner a reason to go think about it. Add tiers after ~10 closes, once it is clear
which upsells people actually ask for.

**The care plan matters more than the one-time number.** It funds the entire
outbound stack (~$225/mo) after three clients, resolves the "I'll pay for it
myself" promise cleanly (absorb hosting and domain up front, recover over months),
and lifts year-one value per client from $2,000 to ~$2,700 at 60% attach.

## Case studies — attribution correction

The numbers in the brief (a moving company: 21% more inbound calls, 17 booked moves in
30 days; restoration: 22% more booked jobs) come from a client of the go-to-market
agency Neil works at. They are not Rave's results.

**Anonymizing them does not fix this — it makes it worse.** The problem is
attribution, not naming. And stripping the name removes the one thing that made the
claim credible: the brief's own answer to "this sounds like a scam" is *"point to
real past results with real business names."* An unverifiable "a client booked 17
moves" is exactly what a scam email looks like.

Three exposures: Neil's employment agreement almost certainly covers client data and
confidentiality; the results are not the agency's to give away, let alone Neil's;
and CAN-SPAM prohibits deceptive content in commercial email, which the brief
already commits to operating under.

**Use this framing instead — true, attributable, and stronger:**

> "I spent the last two years at a go-to-market agency running exactly this for
> local service businesses. One moving company we worked with booked 17 extra moves
> in 30 days."

Neil did the work. "We worked with" attributes honestly to the agency while
establishing he was there doing it. It beats the anonymized version because a
specific role at a real agency is checkable — an owner can look him up on LinkedIn.
The credibility comes from *his experience*, which is genuinely his.

### RESOLVED 2026-08-11

Employment agreement checked — no restriction, the project is separate from work.

**Decision: do not name the client, and do not use the borrowed numbers at all.**
Correct call — the results are not Rave's to claim and the client is not Neil's to
name.

### Where the line actually sits

| Claim | Neil's to make? |
|---|---|
| "I have experience doing this work" | **Yes.** His labor, his skill |
| "Here's a client's name" | No. Not his to give |
| "Rave produced these results" | No. Rave did not exist |
| "I've worked on campaigns like this before" | **Yes, worded honestly** |

Describing work done for an employer's clients is a résumé, not theft. What cannot
be done is transferring the result to Rave or naming the client. Do not discard the
experience along with the numbers — "I have done this work before" is true and is
what an owner actually cares about.

### Strategy: demonstration replaces social proof

The brief already argues this: *"offers where we point out something specific and
show a finished example get replies far more often than generic sales pitches —
because the owner can judge it for themselves in seconds, without trusting our word
for it."*

Case studies transfer trust for claims that cannot be demonstrated. Rave CAN
demonstrate — a working design at a live link. A foundation contractor does not
believe marketing statistics anyway; "22% more booked jobs" reads like every other
agency email he deletes.

**Lead with specificity and risk reversal. Keep experience as a one-line background
note. Drop borrowed statistics entirely.** This also weakens the scam objection
rather than strengthening it: unverifiable stats from a stranger increase suspicion,
a live link and a real name decrease it.

### Approved copy

Fill brackets with verified facts only. Tenure and volume figures are placeholders,
not suggestions — confirm the real numbers first.

**Background** (use once, low in the email or on the call, never as the lead):
> "Quick background: I've spent the last [X] working with local service businesses
> at a go-to-market agency. Rave is my own thing."

**Experience, no borrowed numbers:**
> "Most of my work's been with trades — movers, restoration, driving schools."

> "I've built and rebuilt sites for local service companies for [X]. It's the only
> thing I do."

**The ask** — replaces the brief's version, which claimed a result ("that's helped
[type] book 20%+ more jobs"). This claims a design principle instead:
> "I put together a website design for [trade] companies — built around the three
> things that actually turn a Google search into a phone call. Mind if I show you
> what it'd look like for [company]?"

**Specificity — the real proof:**
> "I was reading [reviewer]'s review about [specific detail]. You're clearly great
> at what you do. The site just isn't showing it."

**Risk reversal — carries the most weight:**
> "Costs you nothing to look. If you like it and want it live by next week, I'll
> cover the setup myself."

**Anti-scam, and a genuine differentiator:**
> "I'm not an agency — it's just me. You'd be dealing with me directly, start to
> finish."

Objection #4 is "I got burned before" and #3 is "web people are expensive and slow."
A solo builder who answers his own phone answers both. Agencies are the thing they
got burned by.

**"150+ local service providers" — drop or verify.** If it is the agency's number it
is not Neil's. If he has personally worked on 150+, it can be said.

### Own the proof in four weeks

The 28-day warmup window is dead time for sending and already allocated to building
five sample designs. Add this:

**Build 3 real sites, free or near-free, for businesses that can be named.**
Friends, family, any local connection — or cold-offer three free builds and take the
first three yeses. Agree three things up front:

1. Written permission to use their name and site
2. A short testimonial
3. **Before-and-after numbers**

The third is the one people forget and the one that matters. Record the baseline
BEFORE launch: calls and direction requests from their Google Business Profile (it
reports both), form submissions, quote requests. Measure again at 30 days.

By week 5, when inboxes finish warming and sending starts, that is three named case
studies with real numbers that are entirely Neil's — and more persuasive than the
agency's, because they are local businesses in the same verticals being emailed.

## How leads are found

Standard B2B databases are built for companies with LinkedIn presence, office
staff and a marketing team — precisely who we are NOT targeting. A roofer running
his business from a truck does not exist in those tools.

Source from Google's local business listings instead. Pull: business name, city,
phone, existing customer reviews.

**Reviews are the highest-value field.** Quoting a specific real compliment a
customer left proves we actually looked at the business. It is the strongest
single thing a first message can contain.

Tools: LocalPipe (list pull), LinkedIn (owner names, weak for trades), Claude Code
(sheets + automated lookups), Google Sheets (working list), Vercel (hosting the
personalized sample pages).

## The delivery mechanic

Do NOT build a custom design per business before talking to them. Build **one
strong sample redesign per business type**, up front, once.

1. Build one sample design per priority vertical (restoration, movers, foundation,
   tree service, fencing). One-time cost, done up front.
2. Email owners in that category offering to show it. Same message and design goes
   to everyone in the category. No per-lead work.
3. **Only when someone replies**, customize the sample with their business name,
   city, phone and a real review of theirs. Turn it into a live page for the call.
4. Close on the call. Build the real site within a few days.

Customization only happens for people who already showed interest, so the workload
stays small no matter how many are emailed. At normal reply rates that is a handful
a day.

**Honesty guardrail:** the sample is built for the business *type*, not their exact
business. So the email offers to *show them a design*, never claims their specific
site is already built. Nobody feels misled on click-through.

The ask, verbatim: *"I put together a website design that's helped [type of
business] book 20%+ more jobs — mind if I show you what it'd look like for [their
business name]?"*

Two practical notes: (1) real logos are often unobtainable, business name in clean
lettering works nearly as well. (2) Showing an owner their own real customer review
on the sample is what makes them say "wait, that's actually my business." Single
most convincing detail available.

## Messaging

Short, plain, one idea, always offer something free with nothing owed.

**Subject lines**
- `[job] request` / `[job] job` — blends into a normal inbox, reads like a customer
- `movers needed` / `roof quote` — looks like real demand
- `can I pay you to rebuild your website?` — unexpected enough to open
- `quick thing on [company]'s site` — casual, owner to owner
- `[reviewer name]'s review` — implies we actually looked

**Opening lines**
- **Found-you:** "Was looking for [type] in [city] and found [company]... had a look at your site and saw a few changes that'd win you more jobs from the people finding you on Google."
- **Real review (converts best):** "I was reading [reviewer]'s review on how [specific insight]. Had to reach out — you're clearly great at what you do, but your website is downplaying that."
- **Google listing (easy yes):** "Pulled up [company] on Google Maps and noticed a few things costing you calls. When did you last update your business listing?"
- **Role reversal:** "Can I pay you to rebuild your website? Why? [review line]. Your team's clearly great — the site just isn't doing it justice."

**Close (proven, keep as-is)**
- Ask: one 10-20 minute call to show the design
- No-risk line: "If you like it and want it up by next week, I'll set it up and pay for it myself."
- Follow-up sweetener: "...and I'll send you 5 [local customers] you can turn into paying jobs."

## Negative filters — who to skip

| Risk | Response |
|---|---|
| Owners avoiding paperwork (older, cash-preferring) | Move on fast, don't chase |
| Owners retiring or selling | Skip. Target owners ~35-50 actively growing |
| Already fully booked | Pitch doesn't land. Skip |
| Overcrowded inboxes (HVAC, plumbing) | Start elsewhere, return once proven |
| Seasonal timing | Restoration spikes after storms, movers peak summer, landscaping seasonal. Time outreach just before each busy season |
| Spam placement | Warm accounts properly before volume |

## Campaign concepts

- **A. Free Design Blitz** — never ask for money up front, lead with a finished design. This is the core plan.
- **B. "I'll Pay to Rebuild It"** — cover setup cost. Removes all risk, unexpected. Must ensure the paid build still covers costs.
- **C. Real Review Callback** — quote a real review, then contrast with the weak site. Best for review-rich verticals (movers, restoration).
- **D. Go Where It's Quiet** — deliberately skip HVAC and plumbing for tree service, fencing, paving, foundation. Quiet inboxes plus weak sites means higher reply rates.

## Compliance

Emailing a business with a genuine offer, a working unsubscribe, and immediate
honoring of opt-outs is standard permitted practice in the US under CAN-SPAM. Only
publicly listed business information is used. Non-US targets require checking local
rules first.

Technical guardrails as this scales: don't re-process businesses already handled if
a run is interrupted, and verify every address is real and safe before sending —
critical early on with new accounts.

## Stated expectations

Reply rate: 3-5% is normal for strong cold outreach. The plan is built around not
wasting effort on the other 95-97%.
